Alisa's Research

Medical research comes first on this page. Below it: clinical trials, then what other people living with ALS say helped them, then food and nutrition, then Alisa's own care record. Everything here is to help her neurologists and her care team see what is out there — nothing here is advice, and nothing here is proof that anything works. ALS has no cure.

Please note: Research results and trial listings are information only, not recommendations. Whether any trial or treatment fits Alisa is a question for her neurologists and her care team. The personal stories below are other people's own experiences, not medical advice and not proof that anything works.

1. Clinical Research

New published research about ALS, checked against the real source. Items are added here only after they are verified — an empty space is better than a wrong one.

Trehalose — HEALEY ALS Platform Trial (published June 2025)

Trehalose is a sugar that helps cells clear out damaged proteins. In animal studies it looked promising, so it was tested in people with ALS in the HEALEY ALS Platform Trial: 161 people at 60 clinics across the USA got either trehalose through a drip once a week for 24 weeks, or a placebo. The result was honest and disappointing: trehalose was safe to take, but there was no clear difference in how fast the disease moved compared with placebo. The study authors said it is unlikely to help at this dose. We list it because a clear "no" from a good study is still useful science.

Source: The Lancet Neurology, June 2025, "Safety and efficacy of trehalose in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (HEALEY ALS Platform Trial)." https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40409314/

2. Clinical Trials

Trials that are listed as open now, from the official trial listing. Whether any trial fits Alisa is for her neurologists and the trial team to decide — never for this page.

HEALEY ALS Platform Trial (Master Protocol)

A long-running trial run by the Sean M. Healey & AMG Center for ALS at Mass General Brigham, with the Network of Excellence for ALS (NEALS). One trial tests several new drugs, one at a time, each against a placebo — as one drug finishes, a new one takes its place. It runs at 60 clinics across the USA. The listing shows it as recruiting, with Regimen I (NUZ-001, a pill taken once a day) as the active regimen. To join, a person must be over 18, within 24 months of first weakness, and able to swallow pills and liquids; the trial team checks everything else.

Source: Trial listing for the HEALEY ALS Platform Trial Master Protocol (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT04297683). https://vanderbilt.trialstoday.org/list/print/NCT04297683

3. Personal Experiences

What other people living with ALS say helped them feel better, in their own words as best we can tell them. These are experiences, not evidence — they do not show that anything works.

1. Dagmar Munn

An ALS News Today columnist, diagnosed in 2010 at age 59 and still writing 16 years later. She says a simple daily structure helped her: taking her prescribed medication, mindfulness, gentle daily movement, morning rituals, therapeutic exercises, mental activities, a healthy eating plan, journaling to track symptoms, and resting instead of pushing through pain. She also says connecting with other people with ALS online turned isolation into friendship.

Source: ALS News Today, "How I learned to live — and thrive — with ALS," Aug 19, 2026. https://alsnewstoday.com/columns/how-learned-live-thrive-als/

2. Larry

An ALS Forums member, living with ALS for three years at the time of writing and able to move only his neck and head. He says voice commands, a head array on his electric wheelchair, and devices his brother and son built for him gave him back independence: controlling lights, bed position, TV, door locks, intercoms, and the computer, plus a ceiling-lift pulley to turn in bed and an angled footboard that stopped painful night cramps.

Source: ALS Forums, "Help to improve quality of life," May 14, 2010. http://www.alsforums.com/community/threads/help-to-improve-quality-of-life.11923/

3. RL Davis

An ALS Forums member sharing what he learned after diagnosis. He says faith, keeping notes on how his body reacted to stress and foods, drinking water, eating fresh vegetables and juices, getting enough protein and good fats, and staying active without overdoing it helped him day to day.

Source: ALS Forums, "10 Things I Wish I Had Known About ALS." https://www.alsforums.com/community/threads/10-things-i-wish-i-had-known-about-als.29477/

4. Donna Boring

Diagnosed in 2008 at age 37 and profiled by The ALS Association. She says family and friends she calls "Donna's Divas" stepped in like a pit crew to fill the gaps ALS created, and that laughter, appreciating small things, not letting the disease define her, and taking life minute by minute, hour by hour helped her get through hard days. She also uses an iPad with a voice app to give speeches.

Source: The ALS Association, "Donna Boring: 'Laughing It Is'" (ALS Awareness Month series). https://www.als.org/blog/donna-boring-laughing-it

5. Heidi Tarr Henson

Diagnosed in spring 2021, writing for The ALS Association. She says building up mobility equipment step by step (brace, cane, crutches, walker, then chairs she calls "The Depot") gave her security, and that mindfulness — stopping, being fully present, doing one thing at a time, and being patient when she got stuck — helped her through the hardest moments.

Source: The ALS Association, "Mindful Mobility," Mar 25, 2022. https://www.als.org/blog/mindful-mobility

6. Gregory Martinez

A surfer and father, profiled by UC San Diego Health in April 2026. He says care from a single multidisciplinary ALS clinic team (neurologist, breathing, diet, physical, speech and occupational therapists, pharmacist) helped his quality of life, and that finding a caregiver he trusted made everything easier.

Source: UC San Diego Health, "Living With ALS: A Patient's Story of Care, Community and Hope." https://health.ucsd.edu/news/features/living-with-als-a-patient-story-of-care-community-and-hope/

7. Brooke Eby

Diagnosed in March 2022 and died Oct 1, 2026 at age 37; she shared her life with ALS openly online. She said family support and constant small adaptations made living with ALS "way, way easier" — including her father building a shower pulley when she could no longer reach the faucet — and that humor, honesty, and a support-group community helped her stay connected. A custom wheelchair leash later let her walk her dog Dray again.

Sources: USA Today, Oct 2, 2026. https://www.usatoday.com/story/life/health-wellness/2026/10/02/tiktok-star-brooke-eby-als-journey-dies-at-37/92054323007/ and Chewy, "How Chewy Claus Helped Brooke and Dray Reclaim Their Favorite Tradition." https://www.chewy.com/education/dog/general/brooke-eby-leash-dray-chewy-claus

8. Casey Harrell

Experimental research — not an available treatment.

A climate activist living with ALS who received an experimental brain-computer interface in July 2023 through a UC Davis research team. He says the implant plus eye-gaze correction let him "speak" again, work, go online, and reconnect with friends and family, calling it "nothing short of revolutionary."

Source: The Cooldown, Jun 26, 2026 (reporting MIT Technology Review). https://www.thecooldown.com/green-tech/man-als-brain-computer-interface-communication/

9. Jacob Harper

Diagnosed in March 2022 at age 23 (genetic FUS form) and profiled by BostonSight. He says scleral lenses (large lenses that bathe the eye in fluid) kept his eyes open, moist and comfortable after he lost the ability to move them, which let him keep watching movies, listening to records, going for drives, and staying present with family. Earlier, an eye-gaze computer and a recreated electronic voice had let him email, text and shop online.

Source: BostonSight, "How Scleral Lenses Helped an ALS Patient Maintain Communication." https://www.bostonsight.org/how-scleral-lenses-helped-an-als-patient-maintain-communication/

10. Coleen

Author of the Got bALS blog, living with bulbar ALS in her 40s with a daughter in high school. She says a food processor for pureed meals, a camper van ("Saoirse") where she can prepare and eat food in private while out, and staying active every day (household chores, walking her puppy, yard tidying) helped her keep living her life, and that gentle exercise helped her sleep better and feel less stressed.

Source: Got bALS blog. https://www.gotbals-bulbar-als.com/blog

11. An ALS Forums Member

An ALS Forums member who had lived with ALS for nearly three years when he wrote in January 2013. He lists the things he says made his life better: a blanket support that keeps the covers off his feet so he can get into bed and position his legs more easily, a power wheelchair that greatly reduced his fatigue, daytime naps that gave him rest and energy, transfer boards for moving seat to seat, adaptive eating utensils with big handles, and an iPad he could still use with one finger to surf the web, email, and post on the forum.

Source: ALS Forums, "Devices that help me," January 2013. https://www.alsforums.com/community/threads/devices-that-help-me.21105/

12. Todd Neva

Todd was diagnosed with ALS in 2010, when he was 39. His wife Kristin, who writes about their life together, says keeping his muscles limber through stretching helped him use the strength he had left, and that adaptive technology lets him stay active working on projects on his computer. Above all, she says, what helped was compassion: people showing up, making room for grief, and finding joy together in the middle of a hard time.

Source: ALS News Today, "ALS doesn't have a cure, but compassion helps," by Kristin Neva, Oct 29, 2026. https://alsnewstoday.com/columns/als-doesnt-have-cure-but-compassion-helps/

13. Mark Ryan

Diagnosed with ALS in April 2025 after a long road to answers, including surgery for a suspected spine problem that turned out not to be the cause. He says care from his ALS clinic team at the University of Colorado helped, along with the mobility equipment he got with help from ALS United Rocky Mountain — a walker for short distances and an electric wheelchair for longer outings. He says staying active with physical therapy, leaning on calm and humor, being open with friends and family, and his wife Diane's support helped him keep living fully: traveling, watching his grandson play baseball, and planning a trip to Yellowstone.

Source: ALS United Rocky Mountain, "Living Fully with ALS: Mark Ryan's Story of Resilience, Humor, and Hope," Sep 26, 2025. https://alsrockymountain.org/living-fully-with-als-mark-ryans-story-of-resilience-humor-and-hope/

14. Norman MacIsaac

Diagnosed with ALS in December 2014 at age 51, Norm calls himself the "luckiest of the unlucky." He says ALS gave him more clarity about what matters: focusing on what he still has, equipment that helps his quality of life like a motorized wheelchair and special utensils that let him eat by himself, and his sense of humor. He also says sharing stories with other people living with ALS — in the Facebook group he started, "Focusing on the Positive: Living with ALS/MND" — makes everyone stronger and more able to live life to the fullest. He is open that staying positive does not mean hiding the hard parts: grief, fear, and the physical struggles need to be talked about too.

Source: ALS Society of Canada, "Luckiest of the unlucky: keeping a positive mindset in the face of ALS." https://als.ca/story/luckiest-of-the-unlucky-keeping-a-positive-mindset-in-the-face-of-als/

15. Monica Torres Rivera

A mother of six, originally from Puerto Rico and now living in New York City, who is studying for a master's degree in criminology and criminal justice. She says a portable ramp she received through a Muscular Dystrophy Association equipment grant gave her back her independence: now she can get into buildings, attend classes, take part in her children's lives, and keep advocating in her community. She also says a family getaway weekend with other ALS families let them simply enjoy being together — wheeling across the beach and kayaking for the first time — and that peer support meetings gave her a place to be honest about what she is going through and know she is not alone. She says asking for support is not a sign of weakness, and that accessibility is about dignity and equal opportunity.

Source: ALS News Today, "ALS didn't end my story — it helped me discover my purpose," by Monica Torres Rivera, Sept 9, 2026. https://alsnewstoday.com/guest-voice/als-didnt-end-my-story-it-helped-me-discover-my-purpose/

16. Barbara Bergin-Nader

A retired orthopedic surgeon, rancher, singer and songwriter, writing for The ALS Association. Her first signs were changes in her voice during the pandemic, and it took about six months of specialists before a neurologist gave her the diagnosis. She says what helped her most was her faith — she turned to God in the darkest two days and says life is good — along with leaning into the things she can still do: writing and blogging, playing her guitar, cooking, caring for her ranch, and singing the blues, which she says she can finally feel. She also says support from the Houston Methodist ALS Clinic and The ALS Association has been a real comfort, and she encourages people to stay connected with those living with a terminal illness and keep involving them in life.

Source: The ALS Association, "The Rhythms of Resilience," guest blog by Barbara Bergin-Nader. https://www.als.org/blog/rhythms-resilience

17. Ron Rowe

Diagnosed with probable bulbar-onset ALS in June 2026, Ron writes about his first year with the disease. He says being outdoors is the biggest factor in his quality of life: he still rides his bicycle to the beach and back every morning, gardens, kayaks, and hikes. He says working through bucket-list trips while he still can — a flight over the Yukon icefields with his son, and a 16-kilometre day hike on the West Coast Trail — helped him feel he was still capable of amazing things. He also says his local ALS support group showed him he and his wife are not alone, and that living life to the fullest means noticing the small things: a hummingbird at a feeder, a deer crossing the street, a rainbow.

Source: ALS Society of BC, "Dealing with My Probable Bulbar-Onset ALS Diagnosis — One Year," by Ron Rowe, September 2026. https://www.alsbc.ca/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Dealing-with-a-Probable-Bulbar-ALS-Onset-Diagnosis-Ron-Rowe.pdf

18. Greg Lampe

A professor and university administrator from Madison, Wisconsin, who was diagnosed with ALS on June 28, 2022. He says he chose to live with the disease rather than try to beat it, and to keep doing what he wants to do for as long as he can. He says what helped him was peace and comfort from family and friends, hope and grace in his faith, a daily routine of strengthening exercises, whole-body stretches and walks, and feeding his muscles with protein and more calories. He also says joining several ALS support groups was one of the most important things that happened since his diagnosis.

Source: The ALS Association, "Living with ALS by Greg Lampe," June 20, 2025. https://www.als.org/blog/living-als-greg-lampe

19. Scott McAdoo

Scott's ALS began in October 2024 with breathing trouble when lying down, and after months of tests he was diagnosed in April 2025. He says one of the brightest parts of his journey has been finding support through ALS Northwest: his local support group, where he meets other people living with ALS and their caregivers to lean on and learn from one another, and a volunteer named Felicia, who visits once a week — helping with household chores, and just as importantly, spending time chatting and laughing together. He says no one should face ALS alone, and that being around people is what makes his time meaningful.

Source: ALS Northwest, "Living Well With ALS: Scott McAdoo," Oct 14, 2026. https://alsnorthwest.org/living-well-with-als-scott-mcadoo/

20. Mark Hustins

Mark spent 32 years serving with the RCMP and retired in June 2019. A few months later he began losing strength in his right arm, and after months of appointments and even shoulder surgery, it did not improve. In November 2023 he received a probable diagnosis of ALS, confirmed in February 2024. His ALS mainly affects his hands and arms. He says ALS took away two of his greatest joys — woodworking in his shop, and music, writing songs, singing and playing guitar — and that losing them felt like grieving the person he used to be. But he says his story is also full of love, humour and resilience: his wife Anne is his caregiver, steady and compassionate on hard days, and he says their relationship has grown even closer through ALS. He says reaching out to his local ALS Society reminded him he was not alone, and that their equipment loan program means the equipment he needs will be there when the time comes. He chose a dragonfly tattoo as a reminder of strength, endurance and hope — hope, for him, is choosing to keep living, to keep showing up, and to keep finding light in the middle of hard days.

Source: ALS Society of New Brunswick & Nova Scotia, "Meet Mark Hustins," 2026 Walk Ambassador story (ALS Walk Strong 2026). https://www.alsnbns.ca/fr/_files/ugd/0cdf98_35ac05b964a8464bbd5428576749d626.pdf

6. Alisa's care record

This is Alisa's own care record, dated November 8, 2026. Dave sent it so her neurologists and her care team can see it. It is saved here as her record. Nothing here is advice, and nothing here says any of this works or should be tried. Whether any of it fits Alisa is for her care team.

Daily supplement schedule

Morning, on an empty stomach: NMN powder, 500 mg (one scoop — the document says to confirm the scoop measures 500 mg); L-Serine, 500 mg (one pill); vegan vitamin D3 + K2, D3 5,000 IU and K2 100 mcg (one pill). After breakfast: sea buckthorn oil, 500 mg; turmeric curcumin with ginger and black pepper (turmeric root 725 mg, turmeric extract 725 mg, ginger root 50 mg, black pepper 10 mg — the document notes these amounts are copied from the product information and may refer to a serving larger than one pill); vitamin C, 1,000 mg; reduced L-glutathione, 500 mg. Lunch: raw probiotic, 100 billion CFU; CoQ10, 100 mg; vitamin C, 1,000 mg; reduced L-glutathione, 500 mg; methyl B12, 5,000 mcg. After dinner: sea buckthorn oil, 500 mg; raw probiotic; turmeric curcumin; vitamin C, 1,000 mg. Daily totals from the schedule: vitamin C 3,000 mg; reduced L-glutathione 1,000 mg; sea buckthorn 1,000 mg; two probiotic pills; two turmeric pills.

The document itself says the quantities and timing are recorded as provided, not independently verified or medically prescribed. It says a clinician or pharmacist should check the full schedule for interactions and dosing. It notes vitamin D3 at 5,000 IU a day is above the usual adult upper limit of 4,000 IU a day unless medically supervised.

Fitness and recovery

Morning breathwork (Wim Hof breathing, 5 rounds — a personal target, not a medical dose), then 20 minutes of sauna with red light therapy. The document warns that ALS can affect the muscles used to breathe, and says not to do the breathing method without clearance from her ALS and respiratory care team — never in a sauna, water, or bath, while standing, or while driving. It also says to get personal advice before sauna use, since heat can worsen fatigue, dehydration, dizziness, and fainting.

Spiritual well-being

Daily personal prayer, family prayer, scripture study, laughter and uplifting shows, and practicing gratitude. The document calls this a personal spiritual routine, not a substitute for medical treatment or clinical care.

The protocol also includes family recipes (Cookbook Favorites). The full document is 25 pages.
